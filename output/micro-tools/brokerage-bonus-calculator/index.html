How the math works

Two sums, one assumption. The first sum is just arithmetic on the offers you enter. The second compounds that total for ten years at an assumed 7% annual return, compounded monthly:

Total bonus = sum of the bonuses you will actually qualify for
Accounts = number of entries above zero
Future value = total × (1 + 0.07 ÷ 12)120

At 7% compounded monthly for ten years the multiplier is about 2.01, so the "if invested" figure is roughly double the bonus. That is the whole point of the third number: a $50 bonus is not $50 of income, it is a small piece of an index fund that keeps working after the promotion is over.

The 7% is an assumption, not a quote. It is a common long-run figure for broad US stock index returns before inflation, and any ten-year window can come in well above or below it. The calculator does not compound monthly contributions, because it is only asking what happens to this one lump of bonus money with nothing else added.

The accounts column is there to remind you that each bonus costs an application, an identity check and usually a funding step.

What the number leaves out

  • Deposit requirements. Many "free stock" offers need a first deposit, sometimes $50 to $250, and some hold it for 90 days. The bonus is not free money if it locks up money you needed.
  • Holding periods. Selling a free share too early can forfeit it. Read the terms before treating the value as cash.
  • Tax. Bonuses and free shares are generally taxable income, reported on a 1099. The calculator shows face value, not after-tax value.
  • Share price moves. A "free stock worth $10" is $10 at the moment it lands. It can be $6 by the time you sell.
  • Account costs. Inactivity fees, transfer fees and minimum balances can quietly eat a small bonus.
  • Your time and credit checks. Every application is paperwork, and some brokers run identity and credit checks.

How to use it honestly

Enter only the bonuses whose conditions you will actually meet — the deposit, the direct deposit, the trade, the holding period. Then treat the total as seed capital rather than spending money: leave it invested, and it is the start of a position rather than a discount on dinner.

Opening accounts you will not use to chase $10 is a bad trade. The better question is which two or three of these accounts you would genuinely keep, and what the bonus is worth on those.

Frequently asked questions

Do brokerage bonuses require a deposit?

Often. Cash bonuses usually require a deposit, sometimes a direct deposit or a minimum balance held for a set period. Some free-share offers only require a signup and an identity check. Check each offer's terms rather than assuming.

Are sign-up bonuses taxable?

In the US, generally yes — they are usually reported as interest or miscellaneous income on a 1099. This calculator does not model tax. Ask a tax professional about your situation.

Can I withdraw the bonus immediately?

Usually not. Free shares and cash bonuses typically come with a holding period, often 90 to 180 days. Withdrawing early can forfeit the bonus or trigger a clawback.

Is the 7% return realistic?

It is a common long-run assumption for broad stock index funds, not a guarantee, and it is before inflation and taxes. Ten years is short enough that actual results can differ substantially.

Should I open every account on the list?

No. Open the ones whose terms you meet and whose platform you would keep using. The $10 offers are worth less than the paperwork they generate.
