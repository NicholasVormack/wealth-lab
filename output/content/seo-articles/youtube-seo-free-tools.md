# YouTube SEO With Free Tools — Titles, Thumbnails and Retention

YouTube ranks videos primarily on whether people click and whether they keep watching. Everything below serves those two numbers. Paid tools help with research, but none of them fix a bad title or a boring first thirty seconds.

## Step 1: Find what people actually search

Free sources that are sufficient for a small channel:

- **YouTube search autocomplete** — type your topic and note every completion. These are real queries, ordered roughly by popularity.
- **The "Search results" filter** — sort by upload date to see whether the topic is saturated with recent, high-view videos. If it is, you need a sharper angle.
- **Google autocomplete** — the same phrases often work on both platforms.
- **Competitor comments** — the questions under popular videos in your topic are a free list of what to make next.

Write down ten candidate titles before choosing one. The tenth idea is usually better than the first.

## Step 2: Write the title and thumbnail together

A title is not a summary. It is the reason to click, stated plainly.

Three title patterns that hold up:

- **Specific outcome** — "How I Cut Editing Time From 2 Hours to 20 Minutes"
- **Direct answer** — "Freelance Rates: What to Charge in 2026"
- **Contrarian** — "Why I Stopped Using a Paid SEO Tool"

Rules that save you from the worst mistakes:

- Front-load the keyword, in the words people search
- Under 60 characters so it does not truncate on mobile
- One idea per title, not three separated by pipes
- No clickbait that the video does not deliver — retention punishes the mismatch

The thumbnail carries as much weight as the title. Text should be three or four words maximum, large enough to read at phone size. High contrast beats pretty. One subject beats a collage.

You can sanity-check both with the [headline analyser](../../micro-tools/headline-analyzer/index.html) and the small screen in mind rather than a laptop.

## Step 3: Descriptions and chapters

The description is mostly for humans, but a clear first line and accurate chapters help.

- **First two lines** — restate the promise of the title, with the keyword naturally
- **Chapters** — timestamps for each section; they show in search and improve navigation
- **Links** — one to your own related content, one relevant external resource, nothing else

Do not stuff keywords. Modern ranking does not need it, and readers notice.

## Step 4: Retention is the real SEO

If viewers leave in the first thirty seconds, nothing else matters.

- **Start with the payoff or the problem** — not an intro sequence, not a logo, not "welcome back"
- **Show the result early**, then explain how
- **Cut every pause** in editing
- **End on a specific next video** rather than a generic outro

Use the retention graph in YouTube Studio — it is free and more useful than most paid analytics. Look for where the line drops, then remove that section from the next video.

## Step 5: Publish consistently and study your own data

Small channels win on volume and iteration, not production value.

- Publish on a schedule you can keep for three months
- After ten videos, compare click-through rate and average view duration across them
- Double down on the format with the best retention, not the best views

## What to ignore

- Subscriber count as a success metric
- Paid view services, which damage the channel
- Keyword density advice from a decade ago
- Chasing trends unrelated to your topic

## Bottom line

Find real search queries for free, write a title and thumbnail that promise one specific thing, deliver it in the first thirty seconds, then read your own retention graph and repeat. That is the entire free playbook, and it beats any paid tool used badly.
