How the math works

An hourly rate is not a salary divided by hours. It is revenue, and revenue has to cover tax and business costs before any of it becomes take-home pay. The calculator works backwards from the take-home figure:

Revenue needed = (take-home target + business expenses) ÷ (1 − tax rate)
Billable hours per year = billable hours per week × 50
Hourly rate = revenue needed ÷ billable hours per year

The division by (1 − tax rate) is the step most people skip. If you want $60,000 after 25% tax, you do not need $60,000 plus 25% — that would leave you short. You need $60,000 ÷ 0.75 = $80,000, because quarter of $80,000 is $20,000. The same logic applies to the broader rate you actually pay once self-employment tax and state tax are included.

Fifty weeks, not 52, is deliberate: two weeks a year disappear into holidays, illness and the days between projects. A rate built on 52 weeks assumes you never stop, and it breaks the first time you take a week off.

What counts as a billable hour

Only hours you can invoice. Not proposals, not discovery calls you lose, not invoicing, not learning the client's tool, not the third revision nobody scoped. A quick test: if you worked 40 hours last week, how many appeared on an invoice? That is your billable figure, and it is usually lower than people assume — often 50% to 65% of the working week.

Set the rate from the honest number. Then if a client asks for a fixed fee, divide the fee by your estimate of billable hours and compare it with the rate. A $3,000 project you expect to take 45 hours is $66.67 an hour, which may be below your floor even though the invoice looks large.

What the number leaves out

  • Self-employment tax. In the US this sits on top of income tax. If your 25% figure is only income tax, raise it or the rate will come out low.
  • Benefits you now fund yourself. Health insurance, retirement contributions and paid time off were part of a salary. Put them in expenses or in the income target.
  • Payment lag. Net-30 terms mean this month's work pays for next month's rent. The rate does not fix cash flow.
  • Scope creep. The rate is a floor for a defined scope. Unbilled extra work quietly cuts it.
  • Utilisation. Fifty weeks assumes you can fill them. New freelancers often bill 50% to 70% of that in year one.

Frequently asked questions

Why divide by (1 − tax) rather than adding the tax on top?

Because tax is charged on the revenue, not on your target. Adding 25% to $65,000 gives $81,250, and 25% of that leaves you $60,938 — close, but it drifts further as the tax rate rises. Dividing is exact at any rate.

Should the income target be gross salary or take-home?

Take-home: the amount that reaches your bank account. Expenses and tax are handled separately, so including them twice would inflate the rate.

Why 50 working weeks?

Two weeks a year for holidays, illness and gaps between clients. If you genuinely bill 52 weeks, change the assumption in your own maths — but most people who claim 52 do not.

Do I include health insurance and retirement?

Yes, and the simplest place is annual business expenses. They are real costs of being self-employed, and leaving them out produces a rate that funds the work but not the person doing it.

My market will not pay this rate. What now?

Three levers: lower the income target, raise billable hours (usually by cutting admin or raising prices to reduce volume), or change what you sell so the work commands more per hour. Dropping the rate below your floor is not one of them — it just moves the shortfall to your household budget.
